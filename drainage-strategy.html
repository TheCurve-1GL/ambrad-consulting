Surface water drainage strategies and SuDS for planning

A drainage strategy shows the council where rainwater goes now, where it will go after development, and how sustainable drainage (SuDS) keeps run-off under control. We prepare it on its own or as part of a Flood Risk Assessment.

Why it matters

Drainage is checked more closely than it used to be

Councils, acting as the Lead Local Flood Authority, review surface water drainage on major development and in areas with known drainage problems. Many now ask for a drainage strategy on smaller schemes too, and in London the London Plan expects sustainable drainage on all development.

Applications get held up when the run-off rates, the discharge route or the maintenance arrangements aren't shown. We set them out clearly, with the working, so the officer and the drainage engineer reviewing it can agree quickly.

Surface water drainage strategy

What the strategy covers

01

Existing drainage

How the site drains today, where water discharges, and the existing run-off rates.

02

Sustainable drainage (SuDS)

The right mix for the site, such as green or blue roofs, permeable paving, rain gardens and storage, following the drainage hierarchy.

03

Run-off rates and storage

Proposed discharge rates against greenfield or betterment targets, with storage sized for the design storm plus the climate change allowance on rainfall.

04

Maintenance and management

Who looks after each part of the system, what they do and how often, so the drainage keeps working for the life of the development.

Questions

Common questions

When do I need a drainage strategy?

Usually for major development (for example ten or more homes, or 1,000 square metres or more of new floorspace), for sites in areas with critical drainage problems, and wherever your council's local policy asks for one. Check your council's validation list, or ask us.

What is SuDS?

Sustainable drainage systems. Instead of piping rainwater straight to the sewer, SuDS slow it down, store it, let it soak away or reuse it. Green roofs, permeable paving, rain gardens and attenuation tanks are common examples.

What is the drainage hierarchy?

The order of preference for where rainwater should go: reuse it, let it soak into the ground, discharge it to a watercourse, then to a surface water sewer, with a combined sewer as the last option. The strategy explains which options suit your site and why.

Can the drainage strategy go in the Flood Risk Assessment?

Yes. For many smaller schemes it sits as a chapter of the FRA. Some councils prefer a separate report, and we can provide either.
